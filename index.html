How This Simulation Works

Everything below is exactly what the simulator computes — no numbers are hidden or hand-picked after the fact. Where something is an estimate or purely cosmetic, it's labeled as such.

1. Airframe constants

Mass1.2 kg
Gravity9.81 m/s²
Motor arm offset (from center of mass)0.16 m
Max thrust per motor5.4 N
Yaw reaction-torque constant0.02 · thrust
Protective cage radius (= collision hull)0.35 m
Assumed impact stopping time0.05 s

These are the only free parameters chosen by hand. Everything else — hover throttle, torque, trajectories — is derived from them by the physics engine, not scripted.

2. Hover throttle (derived, not guessed)

HOVER_THROTTLE = (mass × gravity) / (4 × max_thrust_per_motor)
= (1.2 × 9.81) / (4 × 5.4) ≈ 0.545

This is the exact commanded throttle at which total thrust equals total weight — the PID loop then trims around this baseline instead of discovering it from scratch.

3. Control loop (PID gains)

LoopPIDTarget
Altitude0.650.080.85commanded altitude
Roll / Pitch0.6—0.150° (level)
Yaw——0.02angular-rate damping only

4. Motor mixer

FL = throttle − roll + pitch + yaw
FR = throttle + roll + pitch − yaw
RL = throttle − roll − pitch − yaw
RR = throttle + roll − pitch + yaw

Each motor's output is converted into a real upward force applied at its physical arm position via applyLocalForce. Because the force acts away from the center of mass, the physics engine's own solver computes the resulting roll/pitch torque as τ = r × F — this is not hand-animated. Yaw comes separately, from alternating CW/CCW motor spin-reaction torque proportional to each motor's thrust.

5. Impact G-force estimate

G-force ≈ Δv / (g × stopping_time)
stopping_time assumed = 0.05 s

Impact velocity (Δv) is real — read directly from the physics engine's collision event. The G-force conversion is a standard simplified impact-mechanics approximation, not a measured quantity, since there's no crumple/deformation model. This is the one number in the incident report that's explicitly an estimate.

6. Failure modes — what actually breaks

CategoryMechanism
Motor / ESC / structuralA motor's commanded thrust is zeroed, randomized, or scaled directly in the mixer output
Power (battery / thermal)All four motors' thrust decays exponentially over time
Sensors / softwareCorrupted values are fed into the controller's own feedback loop, so the resulting instability is emergent, not scripted
Link / firmwareThe control loop is frozen open-loop; a small unmodeled airframe-asymmetry torque then causes a gradual, uncorrected tumble
Environment (wind)A real external force + torque is applied directly to the rigid body
Human factorThrottle command is manually forced down

7. Derived inertia (moment of inertia)

Point-mass-at-arms model — each motor ≈ mass/4 at radius ARM:
I_roll = I_pitch = mass × ARM² ≈ 0.0307 kg·m²
I_yaw = 2 × mass × ARM² ≈ 0.0614 kg·m²  (diagonal motors sit farther from the yaw axis)

This is an independent, simplified estimate — cannon.js computes its own inertia tensor internally from the actual box + sphere collision shapes for the real simulation. These derived values are only used to drive the autonomous recovery controller's gains and the physics-telemetry readouts in the log panel, and are labeled as an engineering estimate rather than the solver's internal number.

8. Aerodynamic drag (applied, not just displayed)

F_drag = ½ × ρ × C_d × A × v²
ρ (air density) = 1.225 kg/m³   C_d = 0.9   A (frontal area) = 0.045 m²

This quadratic drag force is computed from the airframe's real horizontal velocity every physics tick and applied directly opposing that velocity — it's a genuine force the rigid body must fly against, not a cosmetic number. It's small at hover (near-zero horizontal speed) and becomes meaningful during a tumble, a wind-gust failure, or free fall.

9. Autonomous post-crash recovery

After a crash, the airframe doesn't just sit there — it self-rights and relaunches on its own, exactly like the rest of the simulation: with real applied torque, not a scripted flip animation. The report from the crash is captured immediately but held back; it only becomes available (📋 LAST REPORT, top bar) once this recovered flight lands safely.

up_world = quaternion × (0,1,0)
angle_err = acos(up_world.y)   (0 when upright)
axis = up_world × (0,1,0)
correction_torque = axis × K_p − angular_velocity × K_d
K_p = 2.4 N·m/rad   K_d = 1.6 N·m/(rad/s)

The gains are deliberately damping-heavy (K_d well above K_p relative to the airframe's inertia) so the maneuver settles smoothly instead of overshooting and oscillating back and forth. The controller keeps driving this corrective torque every tick until the tilt error stays under 10° and angular velocity stays under 1.2 rad/s for a sustained 0.3s — a 5-second timeout forces a handoff regardless, so an unusually stubborn tumble can never hang the simulation. At that point physics stops driving the rotation and a short, eased 0.6s glide (a quaternion slerp with cubic ease-out) smoothly finishes leveling the airframe out and locks it, rather than teleporting instantly — asymptotically settling the last few degrees under real ground-contact friction can jitter indefinitely, so the torque-driven motion (genuinely real physics, from tumble to nearly-upright) hands off to this brief animated finish for a clean result without a visible snap. The cage (rendered from the body's real orientation while tumbling) eases to its locked angle over the same glide, instead of continuing to spin, and stays locked through the ARM → TAKEOFF sequence that follows — which also clears the failure-mode selector back to "random," so a fresh flight never inherits the scenario that caused the last crash. Every stage — angle error, angular velocity, and applied correction torque — streams live to the telemetry log panel (/recovery/attitude) so the reorientation is fully auditable, not a black box.

10. The incident report's mission timeline

Every phase transition for a flight — arm, takeoff, reaching hover, a triggered failure, the crash impact, recovery starting and locking in, the autonomous relaunch, and the final landing — is timestamped and recorded as it happens. The incident report doesn't just show the crash snapshot: it shows the whole story, from takeoff through failure, crash, stabilization, relaunch, and landing, in order. The timeline only clears on a fresh manual ARM from a full reset, so if a second failure happens before landing, both cycles appear in one continuous record.

12. The telemetry log panel

The log panel is styled after real flight-controller/ground-control conventions (topic and node names you'd recognize from an actual system) for authenticity, but it runs entirely client-side in JavaScript — there's no real controller process, no network socket, and no external connection behind it. Where a logged value maps to something the simulation actually tracks (altitude, vertical speed, thrust, torque, recovery angle error), it's the real number; everything else is generated narrative flavor.
